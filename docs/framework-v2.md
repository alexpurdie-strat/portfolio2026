# Premium Product Designer Portfolio — Analysis Framework (v2)

## Purpose

You are analysing product designer portfolios, and related sources, to build a cumulative understanding of two things:

1. **Maturity** — what makes a portfolio read as senior, credible and hireable to hiring managers, design leads and creative directors.
2. **Creative Signature** — what makes a portfolio feel original, inventive and unmistakably authored: the creative genius and innovation that separates a memorable designer from a merely competent one.

These are different axes. A portfolio can be highly mature and generic, or highly original and immature. Score both, separately, every time.

Your goal is not to praise. Separate what is genuinely effective from what merely looks polished. Name the specific, transferable moves behind each effect. Identify weaknesses even in excellent work. After each analysis, update the Benchmark Library and the Cross-Portfolio Patterns sections at the bottom.

---

## Guardrails (always apply)

- Label every observation **OBSERVED** (seen directly in content, metadata or a rendered view) or **INFERRED** (deduced from structure, asset names, context).
- Never state colours, fonts, layouts or visual effects you have not observed. Metadata values (e.g. `theme-color`) count as observed but should be noted as metadata.
- If a site cannot be read (JavaScript-only, blocked, password-protected), mark it **NOT ANALYSED**, record what little is visible, and request screenshots. Do not fill gaps with assumptions or with results about similarly named people.
- Record hygiene issues every time: placeholder text, stale role information, dead or misdirected links, boilerplate or auto-generated metadata, broken embeds.
- Distinguish the portfolio's *intended audience* (product hiring manager, agency creative director, brand client, peers) before judging it. Minimal reasoning is a weakness for one audience and a strength for another.

## Source types and protocols

### A. Individual portfolio

1. Fetch the homepage, every featured case study (up to four), the About/Info/Timeline page, and any secondary pages (work index, writing, experiments, photos, links).
2. If a browser is available, capture: hero at desktop width, top of one case study, a mid-case-study section, homepage at mobile width. Record hex colours, type families and weights (inspect CSS), and motion behaviour (scroll effects, hover states, transitions, autoplay).
3. Count: featured case studies, total projects listed, media assets per case study, approximate words per case study, number of sections.
4. Follow outbound links that carry evidence (published essays, shipped products, press) at least one level deep.

### B. Company design writing (e.g. a design team's essay on a product)

Treat as a reference for how the best teams articulate design strategy. Capture:
- The opening hook and how tension is established.
- Named concepts and principles (coined vocabulary).
- Specific decisions and the rationale for each.
- Trade-offs and ideas deliberately killed.
- Credit given to other disciplines.
- How unresolved tensions and future uncertainty are handled.
- Author, and whether the author's personal portfolio echoes the same principles.

Output: a "Writing Model" entry describing the transferable structure for a case study or essay.

### C. Curated repository (e.g. a directory of portfolios)

Treat as a **norms baseline**, not a ceiling. Capture:
- The curator's editorial criteria, extracted from their annotations (build a lexicon of praised qualities).
- Frequency of each praised quality across the collection.
- Commercial incentives of the curator (courses, reviews, job boards) and how they might bias selection.
- Two or three standout entries worth analysing individually later.

Output: a "Curator Lexicon" entry listing what conventional excellence currently looks like.

## Layer 1 — Maturity: the 16 dimensions

For each, capture evidence, judge effectiveness, extract a transferable pattern.

1. **Positioning and the first five seconds.** Hero copy, length, person (first/third), whether it states role, differentiator and something human. Is the current role visible?
2. **Credibility signals.** Clients, employers, awards, press, appearances. Logo wall, text list, ledger, inline links, or absent? Linked proof or assertion?
3. **Information architecture and content tiers.** Navigation, page model (single vs multi), descending depth (featured → index → experiments → personal), and how each tier serves a different reader.
4. **Volume and curation.** Deep case studies vs lighter entries. Different facets of ability or repetition? Any padding?
5. **Case study anatomy.** Section order, opening device, placement of context (client, duration, team, role), weight of process, ending.
6. **Evidence of thinking.** Named constraints, trade-offs, rejected directions, surprising discoveries, a "hard problem" section. Record the single strongest piece of reasoning.
7. **Outcomes and impact.** Metrics, launch results, adoption, users, acquisition, awards, qualitative results. If absent, is that reasonable for the context?
8. **Role clarity and ownership.** What this designer personally did vs the team. "I" vs "we". Collaborator credits.
9. **Imagery and media.** Context photography, mockups, flat screens, flows, video, GIF, prototypes, process artefacts. Hero treatment. Exploration vs workshop theatre.
10. **Motion and interaction on the site.** Autoplay loops vs click-to-play, scroll effects, hover previews, marquees, transitions. Proof of craft or distraction?
11. **Visual system.** Palette, type pairing, grid, whitespace, image treatment, labelling system. Consistency of micro-details.
12. **Captions and micro-copy.** Do captions state decisions or merely label? Can a reader get the rationale from captions alone?
13. **Voice, tone and language.** Register, sentence length, jargon density, case (e.g. all lowercase), person switches, signature devices. Personality separated from reasoning or mixed?
14. **Personality and About.** Dedicated page, timeline, distributed asides, or absent? What personal content appears and does it add signal about taste and curiosity?
15. **Confidential and NDA work.** Hidden, listed-but-locked, abstracted, or available on request?
16. **Wayfinding, endings and hygiene.** Next-project links, back navigation, contact calls to action, availability status, plus maintenance issues.

## Layer 2 — Creative Signature: capturing genius and innovation

### The organising lens: the novelty budget

Borrowed from The Browser Company's design strategy for Dia: every product has a limited budget of novelty — the effort people will spend learning something new to get value. The best products spend it deliberately on the few places that matter and keep everything else familiar.

Apply this to every portfolio:
- **Where does it spend its novelty?** Identify the one or two signature elements.
- **Is everything else familiar?** Navigation, reading and contact should require zero learning. Novelty in the wrong place (navigation puzzles, unreadable type, scroll-jacking) is overspending.
- **Is the spend meaningful?** Does the novel element express something true about the designer (their craft, obsessions, history), or is it ornamental?

### The 10am Tuesday test

Also borrowed from Dia: could a busy reviewer use this portfolio between two meetings, with no learning curve, and leave knowing who this person is and why they're good? Innovation that fails this test is a liability.

### The nine signature dimensions

1. **Novelty spend.** Where the portfolio invests its originality, and whether that spend is focused and meaningful.
2. **Named ideas.** Does the designer coin concepts, principles or vocabulary? Named ideas are memorable, quotable and repeated in hiring debriefs.
3. **Traceable authorship.** Do the same ideas, motifs or principles recur across different employers and projects? A signature that survives context changes is the strongest evidence of genuine authorship.
4. **Principled sacrifice.** Evidence of ideas the designer loved but killed for a principle. Proves judgement, not just generation.
5. **The portfolio as a medium.** Does the site itself demonstrate the craft or the person's obsessions (e.g. a site element that mimics their creative tools, a voice device that performs their humour)?
6. **Cross-domain transfer.** Visible influence from non-design pursuits (music, photography, sport, illustration, perfumery, type, urbanism, 3D) on how the designer sees and makes.
7. **Shipped self-initiated work.** Products, objects or tools the designer made on their own initiative that reached real users, buyers or press. Distinguish shipped-with-evidence from concept-only.
8. **Published thinking.** Essays, talks, podcasts or principles that expose how the designer reasons, especially those that argue against their own position or acknowledge limits.
9. **Confidence of omission.** What the designer chose not to show. Radical restraint can be a signature, but **only when credentials or shipped evidence are carrying the proof**. Without that support, omission reads as thinness. Always state which is the case.

### Signature scale (score 1–5)

1. **Template** — recognisable platform or theme; interchangeable with thousands.
2. **Polished convention** — well executed, follows current norms, nothing authored.
3. **Distinct voice** — clear personality in copy or visuals, but the structure is conventional.
4. **Signature** — the portfolio would be recognisable with the name removed; novelty is focused and meaningful.
5. **Generative** — introduces ideas, vocabulary or patterns that others quote, adopt or build on.

### Tests to run

- **The blind test.** Remove the name and logos. Would a peer recognise whose portfolio this is?
- **Meaningful vs ornamental.** For each novel element, write one sentence on what it reveals about the designer. If you can't, it's ornamental.
- **The motif trace.** Pick one design idea from the most recent work and search for it in earlier work. Recurrence indicates authorship.
- **The omission check.** List what is absent. For each absence, state whether credentials or evidence compensate.

### Guard against

Mistaking novelty for genius. Mistaking big-name employers for signature (the employer's brand is not the designer's). Mistaking volume of side projects for curiosity (look for depth and shipping). Mistaking minimalism for restraint when it is just emptiness.

## Maturity rubric

**Junior signals:** every project given equal weight; process theatre (double diamond diagrams, personas, sticky notes) standing in for thinking; tool and skill lists; "passionate about" language; long intros before the work; screens without reasoning; step narration instead of decisions.

**Mid-level signals:** clear structure and competent visuals; process described accurately but generically; some decisions explained; outcomes occasionally mentioned; cautious curation.

**Senior / lead signals:** ruthless curation; a named hard problem with real constraints; trade-offs and rejected directions; captions that carry reasoning; outcomes or a credible reason for their absence; clear individual role alongside team credit; a coherent visual and verbal system; confidence without inflation.

**Principal / head-of signals:** the portfolio expresses a point of view; work at the level of product strategy, systems, organisations or brand foundations; evidence of shaping direction; published thinking; the site's restraint is itself the argument.

**Observed pattern (from the benchmark set):** explanation tends to decrease as seniority increases. Senior practitioners with strong credentials show less and write less per project; mid-level portfolios in curated repositories explain the most. This is a description, not advice. Recommending restraint to someone without compensating credentials is a mistake.

## Portfolio archetypes

Classify each portfolio into one or more:

- **Craft-led / art direction** — wins on visual execution, motion, taste and voice. Outcomes often light.
- **Outcome-led / product strategy** — wins on framing, metrics, business impact. Visuals serviceable.
- **Systems / platform** — wins on scale, design systems, cross-team influence.
- **Writer-designer** — wins on published thinking; case studies are supporting evidence.
- **Ledger / principal minimalist** — a sparse record of employers, shipped features and dates; credentials and writing carry the weight.
- **Studio** — presents as a practice with services, clients, press and availability; image-led with little narrative; audience is clients buying taste.
- **Builder / maker** — leads with self-initiated, shipped things (apps, games, objects), often built with code or AI tools; signals curiosity, velocity and end-to-end ability.
- **Generalist** — range across product, brand, motion, 3D, illustration, unified by a sensibility.

---

## Red flags

Placeholder or template text ("Lorem", test headings); stale role or employer information; dead or misdirected links; boilerplate or auto-generated metadata; case studies behind unexplained passwords; inconsistent labelling; walls of undifferentiated screens; unverified metrics; process longer than solution; a hero that doesn't say what the person does; an About page that reads like a CV; novelty in navigation; JavaScript-only rendering with no fallback content (hurts search, sharing and accessibility).

---

## Output format for each portfolio

1. **Snapshot** — name, URL, date analysed, source type, archetype(s), apparent level, intended audience.
2. **Inventory** — counts from the protocol.
3. **Maturity scorecard** — table: dimension, score (1–5), key evidence (OBSERVED/INFERRED), transferable pattern.
4. **Signature scorecard** — novelty spend (where, meaningful or ornamental), scores for the nine signature dimensions, overall signature scale (1–5), results of the four tests.
5. **Signature moves** — the two to four things that make it distinctive.
6. **Weaknesses** — what a demanding design leader would criticise.
7. **Steal list** — copyable patterns ranked by transferability, each flagged with the conditions under which it works.
8. **Verdict** — three or four sentences: what it proves, and for which role and audience it is most persuasive.

## Writing Model — The Browser Company, "The strategy behind Dia's design" (June 2025) · Company design writing

**Author:** Charlie Deets (see Entry 2).

**Structure worth copying for any case study or design essay:**
1. **Open with the paradox.** The working title is revealed as a question about why an innovative company would build a "boring" product. Tension first.
2. **Frame with the user's real moment.** A named scenario (switching at 10am on a Tuesday) grounds the strategy in an ordinary, high-stakes moment.
3. **Name the principles.** Familiarity; familiar-but-elevated (craft, convenience, simplicity); the novelty budget.
4. **Show principled sacrifice.** Loved, creative bookmarks bar concepts were set aside for one nobody has to learn.
5. **List specific decisions with rationale.** No update banners (updates happen in the background, with safeguards against data loss); controls revealed on hover to keep the UI calm; profile selector shown only to profile users; ML routing in the navigation bar; page title shown instead of raw URL, with full URL on hover.
6. **Credit other disciplines** where the solution depended on engineering.
7. **Spend novelty visibly** — explain where animation and colour are reserved for brand moments.
8. **End with unresolved tensions** and a company value about not assuming answers.

**Opening image:** an art-historical reference (a Duchamp readymade) sets a conceptual frame before any UI appears.

# Cross-Portfolio Patterns (after 6 analysed sources)

## Consensus patterns
- **Navigation is always simple.** No benchmark spends novelty on wayfinding.
- **Credentials are stated flatly** — text lists, ledgers or timelines, never logo walls.
- **Personality is distributed, not confined to an About page** (editor's notes, timelines, lowercase bios, voice breaks).
- **Captions and one-liners carry meaning.** Whether one line per project (Caleb) or one decision per asset (Phil), short text does the work.
- **Collaborators are credited** (agency, developer, illustrator partner, engineering).
- **Hygiene slips even at the top** (placeholder text, misdirected links, stale metadata) — a consistent, fixable weakness.

## Divergent strategies (and when each works)
- **Explain vs omit.** Repository favourites explain decisions and outcomes; principals (Charlie, Tracey) and studios (Caleb) omit. Omission works only when credentials or shipped evidence carry the proof, or when the audience buys taste rather than reasoning.
- **Third person vs first person.** Third person creates editorial distance and humour (Phil); breaking it mid-bio creates warmth and surprise (Røde); lowercase first person signals informality and confidence (Tracey).
- **Case studies vs essays.** Case studies prove execution; essays prove judgement. The strongest principal-level signal is essays that argue with themselves.
- **Client work vs self-initiated work.** Big-tech designers lead with client or employer work; makers lead with their own products. Self-initiated work with real users (MOON, Etsy cards, a typeface with press) can outweigh client logos for signalling originality.

## Signature patterns
- **Focused novelty spend** — each benchmark invests originality in one or two places: voice (Phil, Røde), the timeline (Charlie), moving image and owned IP (Caleb), making (Tracey).
- **Traceable authorship** is the strongest originality signal found so far (Charlie's colour-into-chrome motif across Apple and The Browser Company).
- **Named ideas travel.** Coined vocabulary (novelty budget, familiar-but-elevated) spreads beyond the author and becomes a public credential.
- **Cross-domain lives are made visible** as evidence of sensibility, not hobbies: music, photography, illustration, perfumery, type, sport, urbanism.
- **The builder shift.** Designers shipping small products end to end with code and AI tools are emerging as a distinct, high-signal archetype.
