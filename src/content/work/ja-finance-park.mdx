## Why it mattered

Finance Park is Junior Achievement's capstone. Students are handed an adult
persona — a job, a salary, a credit score, a family — and asked to build and
balance a month's household budget. It runs as a 4.5-hour live simulation for
7th to 10th graders, and JA puts the program at around half a million students
a year across the United States. That is JA's reach, not mine. My work is the
platform those sites run on.

The previous platform had not been executed well and was in poor shape. JA had
promised its network a rebuild, and wanted to use it to raise consistency and
quality across 99+ locations at the same time.

What made that urgent rather than merely overdue: the relationship between
JA's national team and the ELC teams who actually deliver the simulations was
already strained, the relaunch was funded by an Eli Lilly grant, and the thing
at risk if it went badly was a worse experience for students at the exact
center of JA's purpose.

## The hard part

I joined three months into the timeline. The team was spinning its wheels on
user flows and could not find alignment.

They were a genuinely talented web platform team whose expertise was mostly
marketing sites, which meant they approached the work page by page rather than
by function — and this was a 120-page interactive product with nine months to a
pilot. Around that sat native iOS and Android requirements, regulatory
alignment tied to the grant, a large and siloed client organization going
through its own transformation, and a legacy platform with a decade of
assumptions attached to it.

The hardest constraint was not technical. As agency of record we had
contractual commitments, and the client carried strong preferences formed by
their past experience of this product. Those preferences regularly ran against
what we would have recommended. Different stakeholders in an organization
mid-transformation meant the narrative of what mattered kept moving.

The RFP arrived as a list of features to build. I reframed it as a prioritized
backlog of grouped feature sets, and worked the groupings until the client was
willing to sort them into MVP, alpha and beta — separating what the product
needed from what people wanted.

The team had never worked this way. To some of them it read as a slowdown, and
it was not a popular move. I was the newest person on a stalled project and had
very little relational capital to spend on it. But once effort had somewhere to
focus, the wheels found ground, and we moved from arguing about flow documents
into wireframing, prototyping and actually designing the thing.

**The alternative I rejected** was the obvious one: accept the shopping list and
work through it in order. That would have spent the same nine months treating a
brand moment and a core budgeting flow as equally urgent, which is how the
first three months had already gone.

### What I killed

I come from agile. The team ran linear, phase-based delivery, and I could not
have both.

So we kept the linear phases as deployment batches and applied an MVP-first
hierarchy *inside* each one. A waterfall-agile hybrid, and not one I would
defend in the abstract — but shifting the team from a page frame to a feature
frame was most of the lever I needed, and insisting on the rest would have cost
me the part that mattered.

### Ask why, not what

Students hit a lock screen while their classmates caught up to the same point
in the flow. We wanted that screen to earn its keep: education, partner
moments, something interactive.

The client kept pulling it back. Eight rounds later we landed on a
dopamine-driven game moment, and internally that felt like a loss.

It was not. Their preference was consistent the whole way through — keep
students engaged and on task, don't hand them a reason to look somewhere else.
We had been asking stakeholders *what* they wanted on that screen and getting
answers we then argued with. When I started asking *why* instead, the
underlying need came out in one conversation rather than eight, and later
features aligned in far fewer rounds.

## Where research changed the plan

The client consistently assumed a more juvenile mindset in their students than
our research supported. We kept finding evidence for a more capable audience
and kept designing against a preference for a simpler one.

In an agency relationship that tension does not always resolve in research's
favor, and it often did not here.

## What went wrong

The real lesson was not about the product.

I joined as the product lead on a team that believed its process was already
sound. They had not built anything this complex before and read their
difficulty as a function of the size of the task rather than the shape of the
approach. I could see blind spots in it, and I held my perspective back until I
judged it would carry — but influencing people who have not asked to be
influenced is delicate work, and under the pressure of being a new hire on a
stalled project I did not treat it as delicately as I should have.

**What I would do differently:** secure buy-in before enacting the change
rather than letting results earn it retroactively. I have been circling an idea
I call *empowering the expertise in the room* — contextualizing a change so
that the people affected can see their own hand in what it makes possible. I
applied it to the client. I should have applied it to my own team first.

**And something concrete that didn't survive:** my final wireframes for the
summary and rewards section that closes the experience. Deadline pressure, plus
unspoken client assumptions my process had not done enough to surface, produced
a wave of late change requests — which culminated in my wireframes being
modified by AI, badly, by someone else. We shipped the client's vision in the
high-fidelity comps. The frustration is real but the cause was mine: a client
who could not see their own expertise in the work.

## Outcome

- Full strategy, design and build in **10 months**, pilot at **9**
- A **120+ page** interactive experience
- Piloted **April 2026** with **10,000+ students**; full release **August 2026**
- Running in **99+ ELC locations**
- Shipped in three stages — pilot, alpha, full release
- Unlocked headcount for continued simulations and reduced strain on the ELC
  teams, which opens the door to expanded operations
- Led to the client signing a further **$1M engagement** with the agency
