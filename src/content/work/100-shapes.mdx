## Why it mattered

I took the Head of Design role at 100 Shapes because I asked for it to exist.
The agency had talented designers and no structure for growing them — no
progression, no shared language for what good looked like, no way for anyone to
know whether they were getting better on purpose or by accident.

An agency lives or dies on whether its people are improving faster than its
clients' expectations. Nobody was measuring that.

## The hard part

Everything I wanted to build was the kind of thing that only works if people
want it, and I was proposing it to a team that had not asked.

You cannot install a growth structure. You can only make one people opt into,
while they are also fully booked on client work, in a business where billable
hours are survival.

## Key decisions

### Start with OKRs, not the ladder

A role hierarchy with skills matrices behind it was the right end state, and it
was months of careful thought away. Waiting for it would have meant a year of
nothing.

So I started with individual OKRs — something I could give a person that week,
that made their own growth legible to them, and that did not depend on the
whole system being finished.

### Two routes up, not one ladder

The ladder came later and landed on ground that had already been prepared.
The decision inside it was refusing a single line: a people-focused route and a
craft-focused route reaching the same seniority, with a specialist path running
alongside both.

Behind the levels sat twelve skills in three families — craft, management and
impact — and five named stages for each, from greenhorn to maestro. The naming
was not decoration. A level called "hot shot" gets used in conversation in a
way that "Level 3" does not.

### Rituals before rules

Weekly critique, pairing, and learning sessions came next, because a matrix
with no shared vocabulary behind it is a document people argue with rather than
use. Once everyone was describing work the same way, the levels meant
something.

The cadence was deliberate: one standing ritual on each Friday of the month, so
nobody had to remember a schedule and no single week carried all of it.

### Managing for strength, not for uniformity

I lean on Todd Henry's framing: creators want the blank page, optimizers take
something decent and make it excellent, fixers get to the root of a problem
faster than anyone and need help knowing when to stop. And on his point that
people need stability and challenge in balance — without stimulating work they
stagnate, without stability they burn out.

I am most comfortable surrounded by designers who are better at the craft than
I am. That is a staffing philosophy, not a compliment.

## Where it got difficult

Two situations taught me more than the structure did.

A senior designer, meticulous and genuinely one of the best I have worked with,
was mentoring a junior and holding them to his own standard rather than meeting
them where they were. The conversation we needed was that empathy is not only
something you extend to users. You bring people up with an arm around the
shoulder, not by dragging them.

The same designer wanted to lead, and had a hard exterior he could not always
see. The advice that landed was the one I still use: do not force someone
straight across a moving river. Steer slightly upstream and let the current put
them where they were going.

The junior in that pair had a habit of reading slow progress as evidence they
were not valued. They are still in touch with the senior designer. They ask
each other for advice and references.

## What went wrong

100 Shapes dissolved in October 2024, and nothing I built there outlived the
agency.
