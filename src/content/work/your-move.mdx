> Built on day one, not at the end. That is the whole story, and also the
> mistake.

## Why it mattered

Your Move exists to help people grow. The team had reached the limit of what
they could do with the tools they had — content going out through a HubSpot
email sequence — and wanted a platform people could steer for themselves.

The bar was not "make an app." It was that a healthy budget spent on a new
platform had to outperform an email list. That is a lower bar than it sounds
and a harder one to clear than it looks.

## The hard part

Six months from a standing start to a working product, with a content library
that did not yet exist in a form anyone could read.

So we were building the shell and teaching the client how to build what went
inside it, at the same time, over several iterations. And the design and
development resources I would normally lean on were committed elsewhere.

That last constraint is why this project looks the way it does. I picked up
Claude Code and built the prototype myself.

<Aside kind="helped">
  The prototype was close enough to a real product to fool the people testing
  it. That let an organization that had never run user testing run several
  rapid rounds, with me as the only practitioner — and see for themselves that
  feedback could go back into the product the same week.
</Aside>

## Key decisions

### Archetypes over personas

The client arrived with six user types and demographic data for each. My first
ask was which one of the six we were actually for.

> A dissatisfied, overwhelmed reader with low perceived mental capacity.

*— The one we picked, out of six.*

Naming him let us find the pains and motivators of a real person instead of
averaging six. It set the tone for everything downstream: the research briefs,
the design language, the wireframes, the prototype and the build all pointed at
him and not at his five adjacent neighbors.

Two of the other five came along anyway. Three were deliberately left outside
the scope, and naming that is what let us ignore feedback that did not speak to
where we were aiming.

**The alternative** was a fanned-out focus: six problems, solved partially. The
version of that project ships something nobody feels is for them.

### What I killed

The archetype was a person with low perceived capacity, so I argued hard for
short chapters. Lower the effort of each segment, give a sense of progress
early, and signal what is coming — before the reader ever reaches the
substance. Around that: card-swipe feedback instead of a long assessment, and a
picture of the reader built up gradually through micro-branching.

The client's background is publishing, and a build-your-own-adventure structure
was a step of flexibility they could not get comfortable with. We shipped a
longer up-front assessment with framework content that opens into personalized
material after about a week of use.

What went with it: daily check-ins that acknowledged the struggle, the
card-swipe interaction, and micro-learning that coached rather than delivered.

## Where research changed the plan

Where I pushed and lost: a hidden decision-maker kept reopening things we had
locked, while the content itself was still being written. We backtracked
repeatedly. I pushed our team to hold the line and was asked to let the changes
through.

The first round of them genuinely improved the product — they brought clarity
the evolving content needed. After that it turned into over-editing, and the
familiar trap of perfection ahead of progress. We eventually held ground because
the timeline forced it, not because I had won the argument.

What that taught me was about a kind of client rather than about this client: a
client who needs to see their idea alive before they can tell you their value
proposition has changed.

## What went wrong

I let a new process talk me out of an old one.

Tools like Claude Code have made high-fidelity, functional prototyping cheap
enough that you can start there. So I did — working prototypes on day one,
instead of strategic briefs, then wireframes, then a design system, then comps,
then a build.

The mistake was reading the ease of getting to the next step as evidence that
the earlier steps were overhead. User flows, architecture, journeys, emotional
mapping — those are not obstacles in front of the screens. They are how you
arrive at definitional clarity, and I found myself craving it halfway through
and going back for it. I spent real time correcting work I had already refined
with agentic assistance, and better input would have removed most of that.

**What I would do differently:** decide up front which parts of the process
produce definitional clarity, and refuse to truncate those no matter how cheap
the next step has become.

> A poet should not ask AI to write the poem. Asking it to collate the masters
> so you can read them is a different request entirely.

The second half of the project ran that way, and the prototyping got better for
it.

<Aside kind="limit">
  This is the clearest thing I know about where AI fails. It can accelerate
  delivery and it cannot supply definitional clarity — and because it makes the
  next step feel free, it is very good at persuading you that you did not need
  the previous one.
</Aside>

**And what did not ship:** most of it. Not for lack of quality — because we did
not surface the client's assumptions early enough. We had read the brief as an
invitation to push; what they wanted was a better-executed version of what they
were already doing.
